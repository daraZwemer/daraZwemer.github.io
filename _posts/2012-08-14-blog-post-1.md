Prelude
======

Last year, I submitted my National Science Foundation (NSF) Graduate Research Fellowship Program (GRFP) application. In the personal statement, I discussed a personal hardship that motivated me to pursue psychology and policing topics. After I submitted the application, I wrote an X/Twitter thread about how I got through writing the personal statement and including information about a significant loss I experienced. I was honored to receive the NSF GRFP this spring! As such, I wanted to spread my advice to a wider audience through this blog post. 

Introduction
======

For graduate and undergraduate students, especially, many grants seek to fund you as a researcher with your story in mind. When your story involves grief or personal suffering, it can be much more difficult to articulate. 

Tip #1: Center your Growth
------

Firstly, approach the story with how you grew, developed ideas, or were motivated by it in mind. Focusing on the pain it caused can detract from your story - the pain is valid, absolutely, but reviewers want to know what you took away from the scenario. Grief never goes away fully, and you don’t need to have “moved on” from something to include it in your story. However, depicting yourself as motionless isn’t fair to you, as you are clearly motivated and strong on account of your willingness to take on a grant and write about your journey, and just by being here.

Tip #2: Care for Yourself During Revisions
------

Speaking of grief not fully going away, know that the revision process can be very tough if you’ve written about a tragedy. Every time you review your personal statement (which will be many times), you will reread the hard parts of your journey. Give yourself more time reviewing the statement, if possible. Take breaks if it gets heavy. You can also switch to reviewing a more technical document, like a research statement, or pre-filling the application if you feel you need to make progress but do not have the emotional bandwidth to re-read the personal statement.

Tip #3: Remind Yourself of your Story's Authenticity
------

Sometimes writing about losses or hardships for a grant can feel exploitative, especially when discussing grief. However, it is valid that you experienced a hardship or a loss that motivated you to do something about it in your field. That’s a worthy, honorable story to tell. I promise, it’s okay to talk about a loss or story that drove you to try to solve something or contribute to society. That’s genuine, not exploitative.

Tip #4: Be Mindful About the Purpose of Feedback
------

Because your content is so personal, it may also feel like feedback is someone criticizing your story at times. I promise that is not the case. Feedback is helping you to present your story the best it can be presented, not criticizing its content. As always, mentors would not provide thorough feedback if they didn’t care about you and the piece you are writing. They want to help.

Summary
======

So, in sum: Give yourself time to write and reread personal sections. Frame the hardship as a valid motivator. Remember that feedback is for your writing, not your life story. And if you don’t get the grant? You’ve not only gained knowledge in grant writing, you’ve also shown strength in vulnerability and reflected on your story. That in and of itself is valuable.

Writing about a personal hardship in my application was more difficult than I expected, and I want others who plan to follow that path to hear this advice - so please, send this article to any graduate or undergraduate student you think it would help!
